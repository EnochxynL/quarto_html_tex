# Introduction

Humanoid robots hold the promise of operating in environments designed for people, but doing so requires whole-body controllers that span diverse human motion, from locomotion and dance to crouching and balance recovery. Physics-based motion imitation addresses this by training RL policies to track reference motion clips [@peng2018deepmimic] drawn from large motion-capture (mocap) datasets [@luo2025sonic]. Existing systems scale this idea either by distilling many clip-specific experts into one controller [@liao2025beyondmimic] or by training a generalist policy over a large motion library [@luo2025sonic]. In both cases, a more diverse motion library produces a more capable controller. But both also become computationally expensive as motion coverage grows: the state-of-the-art tracker SONIC [@luo2025sonic], for example, trains for $\sim$ 168 h on 128 GPUs, totaling roughly 21000 GPU-hours. Although reference motions differ, they share the same humanoid hybrid rigid-body dynamics. Model-free RL fails to explicitly model this shared structure, instead repeatedly estimating action consequences from noisy simulator rollouts. Scaling to more motions therefore amounts to scaling up compute.

SuperMimic addresses this inefficiency with a single learned, differentiable world model fit from logged simulator transitions. Backpropagation-through-time (BPTT) through short imagined rollouts provides analytic policy gradients through the learned model, allowing one dynamics model to amortize learning across the entire mocap library. Because these gradients come from the learned model rather than from differentiating the simulator, SuperMimic remains compatible with rigid-body simulators, including those with non-smooth contacts and non-differentiable implementations.

On one GPU, SuperMimic surpasses the normalized return of SONIC's released checkpoint within 3 h and reduces GPU-hours by more than $1000\times$ (§\ref{sec-scaling}). On a 129514-motion benchmark, SuperMimic fails on 25 motions after 3 h on one GPU and on none after 9.8 h on two GPUs, against 179 for SONIC's released checkpoint (Appendix \ref{sec-eval}). Across a $264\times$ increase in dataset length, the world model's prediction error stays stable and SuperMimic's compute to a fixed return grows slowly. The learned policy transfers to a physical Unitree G1. In a preliminary study of downstream interfaces, an RL-trained velocity encoder outperforms a distillation baseline.

#### Contributions.

- **Model-based RL (MBRL) for deployable humanoid control**. We demonstrate that BPTT through a learned, differentiable world model can train whole-body tracking policies in a setting long dominated by model-free proximal policy optimization (PPO) [@schulman2017proximal], and that the resulting controller transfers to a physical Unitree G1.

- **Compute is no longer the bottleneck to scaling.** SuperMimic surpasses the return of SONIC, a cluster-scale PPO pipeline, on a single GPU and reduces GPU-hours by more than $1000\times$ (§\ref{sec-scaling}). Its training cost grows slowly as the motion library expands, making scaling humanoid whole-body control practical without huge GPU infrastructure.

- **An analysis of the ingredients behind the gains.** We identify three world-model properties behind the compute reduction: prediction error that stays stable over a $264\times$ increase in dataset length, rollouts more than $10\times$ faster than the simulator, and analytic gradients, with which the policy reaches the best return of PPO on the world model with about $64\times$ fewer imagined steps.

- **RL-trained downstream control beyond distillation.** As a preliminary study, the same learned world model trains a downstream goal encoder on the interface's own task reward. On velocity commands, the policy tracks more accurately with this encoder than with a distillation baseline.
